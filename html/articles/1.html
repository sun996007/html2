Title:How LeBron James Became LeBron James—Again. And Again.

In his historic 23rd season, James remains the only player to withstand aging out of the game he’s loved for almost 40 years. He started in the league as a generational prodigy, explosive and dominant with a natural ability to finish at the rim.

He grew into a champion and MVP who became more deliberate, efficient, and masterful in his post-up game, perfecting his signature fadeaway jumper along the way.

Now, his basketball acumen is unparalleled. His playmaking, which has always been elite, is now downright cunning. He plays against guys who weren’t even born when he entered the league, and not only does he defeat them, sometimes he’ll even chase them down for a block. He’s a three-point threat, too.

Like music and style, there are eras of LeBron James. And it’s more than skill that’s made him iconic.

“There is a separator between truly effective professional athletes and truly outstanding, iconic professional athletes,” Dr. Traci Statler explained.

Statler has over 20 years of experience in sport psychology, with her doctoral dissertation focused on the science behind what separates effective athletes from truly excellent ones. She has worked extensively with elite organizations, including USA Track and Field for more than two decades, served as a consultant for USA Women’s Volleyball during an Olympic cycle, collaborated with USA Cycling—all centered around optimizing mental performance.

Her expertise lies in mental coaching: training the brain, mind, and thought processes to work in an athlete’s favor under high-pressure, high-performance conditions, rather than focusing on physical, technical, or tactical skills. Dr. Statler’s approach is firmly grounded in research, particularly studies that differentiate elite athletes from the “super-elite,” a term she aligns with what many would call iconic or legendary athletes.

Statler conveyed that an athlete’s ability to reach super-elite levels depends on both personal traits and their environment, such as birth order, upbringing, and available opportunities. These early experiences shape how they train and practice. Ultimately, how they use their skills and environment plays a crucial role in their success.

“It takes a perfect storm of all those things to line up in alignment for a truly iconic person to generate,” she said.

For all athletes, there are key personality traits, said Statler, like the ability to handle pressure and manage challenges; these are partly innate and partly teachable. It’s not that they don’t experience pressure; it’s that their perception of pressure is different.

“You definitely train your mind,” LeBron said. “When you set out goals and you want to achieve those goals—or if you want to be known forever, and want to be known as a champion, and want to be known as someone who experienced failure and was able to overcome those failures, you definitely train the mind.”

But while elite and super-elite athletes share a very common mindset, one factor that sets super-elite athletes apart is that they often use a difficult experience in their life as motivation to push themselves even further, Statler noted.

“My upbringing helped train my mind too in the sense of where I come from: me and my mom, a single-parent household, no siblings, underprivileged. Those things also shape you as well,” LeBron said.

“For me, I learned how to deal with adversity and how to handle shortcomings at such a young age that it helped me in my playing career as well. I’ve always had the opportunity to be like, okay well if I lose a game, if I lost a Finals game, as hard as it’s been to digest still to this day, as hard as it is in the moment, as much as it sucks, especially if you don’t think you’ve performed the way you should’ve performed, is that the hardest thing that’s happened in my life? And it’s not.”

While most people see challenges like loss or heartache as stressful, super-elite athletes often turn to their sport as a “safe haven.” It’s a place to regain control, feel better, and focus their energy. But that doesn’t mean they love the game any more than an elite athlete.

In fact, Statler explained that all professional athletes love the game the same. They love it immensely. They have to. It would be too hard to sustain if they didn’t.

“Where some difference lies in the super-elite,” she said, “is that within that performance space, for extreme performers, it’s not about winning the game, success, or accolades. It’s about the need to love the sh-t, the need to love the grind, the need to love the hardest, most difficult, backbreaking workout on your programming sheet.”

As an iconic athlete, you love the game. And you love what people hate about it. That’s what separates you from everyone else.

“The difference on the psychological side is the person who not just embraces a growth mindset and the suck but really gets excited about how hard it is. The ones who leverage difficulties that might paralyze others are the ones who rise above,” she said.

Even with performance, resilience, and tenacity, icons also need personal qualities that resonate beyond stats. “Charisma, sense of humor, humility, thoughtfulness, reflectiveness,” Statler listed.

For LeBron, who’s been under the public eye since he’s been a kid, his every move speculated, “I can only be myself,” he said. “Truly, people are going to recognize if I’m not being authentic. And for me, I’ve always kind of been myself.”

He saw he had to. The realization came in 2010 when he decided to go to Miami.

“I saw a lot of things shift, narratives and people’s minds about a decision that I thought would make me happy. And that kind of shifted my thinking on really truly being yourself and not caring about what everybody thinks—because there’s no way you’re going to make everybody happy,” he shared.

“You have to always understand that you’re making a decision. You live with that decision. You be truthful about the decision to yourself. And the only people that matter are the ones who are literally in your corner.”

And so, LeBron will always show up entirely as himself. He dominates. He dances his way to the top. He’s done it again and again.

Because as Statler puts it, “Truly iconic athletes have found a personal formula for performance preparation that allows them to repeat, replicate, and consistently perform at the highest level. That’s the difference maker—not being great once but being able to do it over and over.”

And then after mastering every realm of the sport, transcending it altogether, they’re still not done. Because more than loving the game, iconic athletes love a challenge, especially LeBron.

“The thing that stands out is that when you get to that level,” Statler said, “you don’t say, ‘I know everything.’ Good enough is not good enough. Your curiosity forces you to look outside your space—even into marine biology or other industries—to learn what makes greats great, so you can be better in your own.”

That’s when you stamp time more than once. That’s how you’re remembered forever.

“I hope it’s a great feeling, you know, when you think of me and you think about what I’ve been able to accomplish. And I hope the basketball point is not the biggest part of what I was able to accomplish.

I hope people look to the things I was able to do in my community, and the people around me who I’ve helped grow, and I’ve helped lead to where they’re doing bigger and better things.

Obviously, there’s going to be some great things that you’ve seen that I’ve done as far as a basketball player, but I hope that’s not the only story. I hope I’m able to bring some joy and smiles to a lot of households and people that follow my career.

And even long after I’m gone, they see what my kids were able to accomplish, because of how myself and Savannah were able to raise them and put them in a position to be successful.

I hope people look to my community, and my hometown of Akron, Ohio, what we’re building there for families and things of that nature. And so many things all over the world where I hope we’re able to inspire.

I hope that I’ve done something right and I hope people can tell that story. None of us are perfect and have perfect attendance, but we can all strive to be the best we can. I hope the story is really well put together,” LeBron said.
